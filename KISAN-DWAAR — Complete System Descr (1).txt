KISAN-DWAAR — Complete System Description  What The System Is
KISAN-DWAAR is a platform that connects Indian farmers directly to bulk buyers (traders, companies, mandis, processors), removes middlemen, recommends a fair price using real market data, groups small farmers together to fulfill large orders, arranges transport through a competitive process where a buyer can bid only one time if he wants and cannot bid back if anyone outbids him, and has a government official verify and sign off every delivery before payment is released. The whole farmer-facing side works over ordinary phone calls (IVR) so that farmers without smartphones or internet can fully participate using just a keypad phone.
The system has four types of users and one government layer:
Farmers — sell crops, interact almost entirely by phone call (IVR), optionally via a basic web view.
Buyers — post demand for crops, use a full web portal.
Transporters — submit a one time bidding offer (where they bid once and cannot re-bid if outbid) to move crops from farms to buyers, interact via a small app or IVR.
Government officials — verify deliveries, oversee the whole system, use a separate secure portal.
The platform itself — runs the price engine, groups farmers into pools, runs the transport auction, and holds money in escrow until delivery is confirmed.  The Four User-Facing Applications
2.1 Farmer & Buyer Web Portal
A mobile-first website/PWA with two sections behind login:
Farmer section: shows the farmer's profile, land details, crops grown, list of current offers received (and their status: pending, accepted, rejected), payment/settlement history, and a language preference setting. Farmers mostly don't need to actively do anything here — most of their real interaction happens over the phone (see Section 5). This section is a passive "status mirror" of what's happening to them.
Buyer section: a buyer can create a new demand (crop type, quantity needed, price they're offering, delivery location, delivery deadline), see which farmer pools have been matched to their demand, track the order status live (forming → confirmed → picked up → in transit → delivered → paid), download invoices, and see analytics on their past purchases.
Works offline for farmers with poor connectivity — drafts are saved locally and synced when back online.
2.2 Government Oversight Portal (completely separate application and login)
Used only by government/mandi officials, on its own separate site with its own separate login — kept structurally isolated from the farmer/buyer system so it can be independently audited and trusted. It shows:
A live dashboard of every pool currently forming, every delivery currently in transit, and every escrow account currently holding money.
A delivery verification screen: when a truck arrives at the buyer's location, the official scans a QR code tied to that specific delivery, and the screen shows what was promised (crop, grade, quantity) next to what was actually delivered (a photo taken at delivery, the weighed quantity). The official presses a "Confirm Delivered / Quality Pass" button. This single action is what unlocks payment release — nothing gets paid out until a government official has signed off.
A complete audit trail: every important action anywhere in the system (an offer accepted, a one time bidding offer awarded, a payment released, a dispute flagged) is permanently logged and viewable here, filterable by farmer, buyer, date, or action type. Nothing in this log can be edited or deleted — it's a one-way record.
A flag/investigate tool so an official can mark a transaction as suspicious, which creates an audit entry and alerts the platform's operations team.
Simple analytics: crop yield trends, demand trends, and price trends over time, to help with policy decisions.
2.3 Transporter App
For transporters who do have a smartphone: shows incoming one time bidding invitations (a pool needs transport from village A to buyer B, X kg, by Y date), lets them submit a single non-repeatable bid (rate), shows their bidding history, and once they're awarded a job, lets them log GPS location as they drive and upload photos at pickup and delivery. Transporters without a smartphone use the IVR system instead (Section 6), so this app is optional infrastructure for fleet operators, not a requirement for every transporter.
2.4 The IVR Voice Layer (no screen at all)
This is not an "app" — it is an actual phone call. The system calls a farmer's or transporter's real mobile/landline number, they hear a voice menu in their own language, and they respond by pressing number keys on their keypad. This is covered in full detail in Section 6, since it's the core innovation of the platform and must be a real, working phone call system — not a simulated or fake call.  The Core Data Objects (what the system keeps track of)
Farmer: name, phone number, verified identity, land size, which crops they grow, their location, preferred language, bank details (encrypted).
Buyer: organization name, business registration/tax ID, credit standing, verification status.
Mandi Price / MSP data: daily market prices per crop per market, and the government's minimum support price — refreshed continuously from public sources.
Demand: a buyer's request — crop, quantity, price they're offering, where it needs to be delivered, by when.
Pool: a group of nearby farmers whose combined harvest can fulfill one demand. Has a status (forming, confirmed, failed) and tracks how much quantity has been committed so far versus how much is needed.
Offer: one individual farmer's personal invitation to join a pool at a specific price — has a status (pending, accepted, rejected, callback-requested).
Transport One Time Bidding Record: a transporter's single quoted rate to move a specific pool's goods (no re-bidding permitted if outbid), with a status (submitted, awarded, declined).
Delivery/Execution record: tracks the physical journey — pickup confirmed, checkpoints along the route, delivery confirmed, quality-checked, government-signed-off.
Escrow account: holds the buyer's payment for one pool until delivery and government sign-off both happen, then releases the money split between farmers (proportional to what each contributed) and the transporter, minus a platform fee.
Audit log: an unchangeable record of every state change in the system, for the government portal.
Call log: every IVR call made — who was called, what happened, whether they accepted/rejected, was the call recorded.  The End-to-End Flow (exactly how a single transaction moves through the system)
Step 1 — Price is always being calculated in the background. The system continuously pulls in live mandi (market) prices and the government's official minimum support price for various crops, feeds them into a price-prediction model, and produces a "fair price" recommendation for each crop/variety/market combination. This fair price is what farmers get told over the phone later, so they can judge whether an offer is good.
Step 2 — A buyer posts a demand. A buyer logs into the web portal and says: "I need 2000 kg of wheat, Grade A, delivered to my warehouse in [location] by [date], I'm offering ₹X per kg." The system checks the buyer is verified (valid business registration, within their credit limit) and saves this demand.
Step 3 — The system finds candidate farmers. It searches for farmers who grow that crop, are within a reasonable driving radius of the delivery point, and whose harvest timing matches. If one single farmer can't supply 2000 kg alone (very common, since most farmers are smallholders), the system looks for a group of nearby farmers whose combined harvest can.
Step 4 — The system groups farmers into a pool. Using an optimization step, it picks the best combination of nearby farmers to minimize total travel distance while meeting the total quantity needed and keeping crop grade/variety consistent. This produces a "pool" — say, 5 farmers who together can supply the 2000 kg.
Step 5 — Every farmer in the pool gets called (real phone call). The system automatically places outbound phone calls to each of the 5 farmers' actual phone numbers. Each hears (in their own language): "Buyer X wants Y quintals of [crop] at ₹Z per quintal. The official fair price is ₹F. Press 1 to Accept, Press 2 to hear the current mandi price again, Press 3 to Reject, Press 4 to be called back later." This is a real, live phone call to a real number — not a text message, not an in-app notification, and not a demo/simulated call — because the whole point of this platform is reaching farmers who don't have smartphones.
Step 6 — Farmers respond by pressing keys. If a farmer presses 1 (Accept), the system asks them to confirm once more, then locks in their committed quantity. If they press 3 (Reject), their offer is marked rejected and the call ends politely. If they press 4, the system automatically schedules a callback in 2 hours and calls them again later. If they don't respond or press something invalid, the system repeats the menu up to 3 times, and after that, transfers them to a real human support agent ("Krishi Mitra").
Step 7 — Quorum check. As farmers accept, the system keeps a running total of committed quantity. Once the total committed reaches a threshold (e.g. 95% of what the buyer asked for), the pool is marked "Confirmed" and moves to the transport stage. If not enough farmers accept, the pool can be adjusted (more farmers invited) or marked failed.
Step 8 — Transport is arranged through a single-round, sealed reverse auction. Once a pool is confirmed, the system broadcasts the job (pickup locations, total weight, delivery point, time window) to nearby transporters — via push notification/app for smartphone transporters, and via a similar real phone call for transporters without one ("Press 1 to quote a rate, 2 to hear details, 3 to decline; then enter your rate per quintal-kilometer followed by #"). Transporters submit a single rate quote; each participant can only bid once and cannot submit a counter-bid if another party submits a lower rate. The system automatically checks each bid against a reasonable rate range (to block absurd overcharging) and against actual route feasibility (using mapping/routing data), then awards the job to the lowest valid single bid.
Step 9 — Physical pickup and delivery. The awarded transporter picks up the crops from each farmer, the app streams live GPS location the whole trip, and photos are taken at pickup and at delivery as proof.
Step 10 — Government verification. When the truck arrives, a government official (using the separate Government Portal) scans a QR code tied to this delivery, compares what was promised versus what actually arrived (weight, photos, grade), and signs off "Delivered / Quality Pass." No payment moves without this human government check.
Step 11 — Escrow release. Once delivery is confirmed AND quality has passed AND the government official has signed off, the system automatically releases the buyer's held payment: each farmer gets paid their share proportional to how much they contributed, the transporter gets paid their winning single bid amount, and the platform takes a small fee. Payments go out via UPI/bank transfer.
Step 12 — Everything is logged. Every single step above — the offer, the accept/reject, the single bid submission, the award, the delivery confirmation, the payment — writes a permanent record to the audit trail, visible in real time on the Government Portal.  Why Voice/IVR Is Central (not optional)
Most of the target farmers use basic keypad phones with no internet. So instead of requiring them to install an app, read text, or use a smartphone, the entire "am I getting an offer, do I accept it, what's the fair price" interaction happens through an actual phone call where they just listen and press number keys — the same as calling a bank's customer service line. This is what makes the platform usable by the poorest, least digitally-connected farmers, who are also the ones most exploited by middlemen today. Because of this, the phone-call system is not a minor feature — it is the primary interface for the majority of users, and it must actually place real calls to real numbers, not simulate the experience in a demo screen.  Full Detail of the IVR (Phone Call) System
6.1 Two kinds of calls
Farmer calls — offering them a chance to join a pool and sell their crop.
Transporter calls — offering them a chance to submit a single, final bid on a delivery job.
6.2 Farmer call — full script and logic
Call connects. System greets the farmer and asks them to choose a language by pressing 1 (Hindi), 2 (English), or a regional language option. If they don't respond after two prompts, it defaults to Hindi.
System plays the offer summary: which buyer, which crop, how many quintals, the price being offered, the government minimum support price for comparison, and the system's own "fair price" estimate.
Main menu is played: "Press 1 to Accept, Press 2 to hear the current local mandi price, Press 3 to Reject, Press 4 to request a callback in 2 hours."
If they press 2, the system tells them the nearest market's current minimum, maximum, and typical price, then automatically returns to the main menu.
If they press 1, the system asks a final confirmation ("You are agreeing to sell X quintals at ₹Y — press 1 to confirm, 2 to go back"). On confirming, it records their acceptance, tells them they'll get an SMS confirmation, and hangs up. This is the moment the system checks whether the whole pool now has enough farmers committed.
If they press 3, it records the rejection, thanks them, and hangs up.
If they press 4, it tells them they'll be called back in 2 hours, schedules that follow-up call automatically, and hangs up.
If they don't press anything valid, the menu repeats. After 3 failed attempts, the call is transferred to a live human support agent instead of hanging up.
Every outcome of the call (accepted / rejected / callback / handed to human) is permanently logged, along with a recording of the call itself, for the government audit trail.
6.3 Transporter call — full script and logic
Call connects, system verifies this is the correct transporter for a specific pool/job.
System reads out the job details: which villages to collect from, the warehouse to deliver to, total weight, the pickup/delivery time window, and the distance involved.
Menu: "Press 1 to quote your rate, Press 2 to hear the details again, Press 3 to decline."
If they choose to quote, they type their price per quintal-kilometer on the keypad, ending with #.
The system checks the rate is within an allowed range (to prevent wildly overpriced or clearly erroneous single bids), and if valid, reads it back and asks them to confirm with 1 or re-enter with 2.
On confirmation, the single bid is saved (no further modifications or counter-bids allowed), and it is evaluated against all other submitted single bids to award the job automatically to the best valid bidder.
If their entered rate was invalid, they get up to 2 retries before the system records it as a decline.
6.4 What makes this "real" and not a demo
A real version of this system requires: an actual telecom voice-calling account (a business signs up with a voice API provider), a real phone number purchased through that provider which becomes the caller ID, the system's backend being reachable over the public internet so the phone provider can send it call events, and the system making an authenticated request to the provider's calling API whenever it wants to trigger an outbound call. When all of this is in place, the system genuinely dials a real phone, the phone genuinely rings, and the person's real keypad presses are sent back to the system live — there is no "fake mode" here; it is the same technology used by banks and delivery companies for their automated phone systems. For Indian phone numbers specifically, this kind of automated calling is also subject to telecom regulations (registration requirements, do-not-disturb list checks, and requiring the person's active consent, which this design already builds in through the explicit "press 1 to accept" step).  The Fair Price Engine (how the recommended price is calculated)
The system continuously ingests: (a) daily government mandi price data for each crop/variety/market across the country, (b) the official government minimum support price for that crop, (c) how much of the crop is currently arriving at markets (supply level), and (d) seasonal patterns. It uses these to predict a fair price for any given crop, variety, and market at any point in time. This predicted price is cached for fast lookup and shown to farmers during their IVR call, and to buyers when they're posting a demand — so both sides can see a neutral, data-backed reference point instead of relying purely on a middleman's word.  The Pooling Engine (how farmers get grouped)
Because most individual farmers can't fulfill a large buyer order alone, the system searches for farmers growing the right crop within a sensible distance of the delivery point, then groups the smallest possible number of nearby farmers whose combined harvest meets the buyer's needed quantity — trying to keep total travel distance low and crop grade/variety consistent across the group. This turns many small, individually-unattractive harvests into one large, commercially viable order.  The Logistics Engine (how transport is arranged)
Once a pool is confirmed, the system runs a single-round reverse auction: instead of back-and-forth bidding, each transporter can submit only one final bid. If another participant quotes a lower rate, the original bidder cannot place a counter-bid. The system checks feasibility (can this vehicle actually complete this pickup-and-delivery route within the required time window) and automatically awards the job to the cheapest valid single-bid transporter.  The Escrow & Settlement System (how money moves safely)
The buyer's payment is placed into a holding account (escrow) as soon as the pool is confirmed, not paid out to anyone yet. It only gets released after three separate conditions are all true: the delivery is confirmed as physically complete, the quality of what was delivered matches what was promised, and a government official has manually signed off on the delivery. Once all three are true, the system automatically splits the payment — each farmer gets paid proportional to how much of the total order they personally supplied, the transporter gets their single winning bid amount, and the platform keeps a small service fee. This protects farmers from non-payment and protects buyers from paying for goods that were never actually delivered or didn't meet quality.  The Government Oversight Layer (why it's separate and what it actually does)
A government official's job in this system is narrow but critical: they are the final, human, independent check that a delivery genuinely happened and met quality standards before any money moves. This is why the Government Portal is kept as a completely separate application with its own login — it's not meant to be just another view inside the buyer or farmer app, it's meant to function as an independent oversight body, similar to how a mandi inspector operates today, except now every action they take (and every action anyone else takes) is permanently and transparently logged for later audit.  Why the System Prevents Common Failure Points
A farmer's harvest is too small to matter to big buyers → solved by the pooling engine combining several farmers into one order.
A farmer doesn't have a smartphone or internet → solved by the IVR system, which needs nothing but a basic keypad phone and a real phone call.
A farmer can't find or afford transport at harvest time → solved by the single-round transporter auction, which collects one-time competitive bids without chaotic multi-round negotiations.
A middleman quotes a lower price than what's fair and the farmer has no way to check → solved by the fair-price engine reading out the real mandi price and MSP during the same phone call as the offer.
A buyer pays but never receives the goods, or a farmer delivers but never gets paid → solved by escrow, which only releases money after delivery + quality + government sign-off are all confirmed.
No accountability or record if something goes wrong → solved by the permanent audit log visible on the Government Portal
